Selling a Rental vs. a 721 UPREIT: A Worked Hypothetical With Round Numbers

Published October 20, 2026 · 721 UPREIT Pros

Numbers make the sell-or-721 decision easier to picture. The example below is hypothetical and simplified, uses federal rates only, and ignores selling costs and debt. Your CPA's math will differ.

The setup

An owner bought a rental years ago for $400,000, claimed $150,000 of depreciation, and can now sell for $1,000,000. That leaves an adjusted basis of $250,000 and a total gain of $750,000.

Path A: sell and pay

  • $150,000 of depreciation taxed at up to 25%: about $37,500
  • $600,000 of price gain at up to 20%: about $120,000
  • 3.8% net investment income tax on $750,000 if it applies: about $28,500
  • Roughly $186,000 federal before state tax, leaving about $814,000 of cash to use freely

Path B: contribute through a 721

If the property, or a DST it was exchanged into, is contributed to an operating partnership, the full $1,000,000 of value generally stays invested as OP units and the $750,000 gain is deferred. The catch: units are less liquid, a 721 UPREIT holding period and lockup applies, and redeeming units later can trigger the deferred tax. See 721 exchange: UPREIT or sell for the qualitative trade-offs.

The usual bridge

Because REITs rarely accept a single rental directly, many owners first do a 1031 exchange into a DST. DST 1031 explained covers how that deferral works, and the DST 1031 exchange guide for retiring landlords covers income and exit timing.
